Electricity Tariffs Explained

If you have ever wondered how electricity tariffs actually works in practice, you are not alone. Most people learn it by trial and error, and trial and error is expensive when money, fuel, electricity or data is involved. A better approach is to understand the three or four inputs that drive the result, test them with your own numbers, and then make one small change at a time. This guide does that for you. It explains the idea in everyday words, shows the calculation in a worked example, and points out the traps that catch beginners. You do not need any special maths background, only a willingness to check your figures before you act on them.

How it works

To use electricity tariffs well, break the problem into three parts. First, work out how much you use or need. Second, work out the rate that applies to that use, remembering that rates can change with the time of day, the amount used, or the provider you are with. Third, combine the two and add any fixed fee. That three-part structure is the same whether you are planning savings, working out a tax amount, estimating a trip, checking a bill, or measuring data use. Keep the parts separate in your head, because most errors come from mixing them up, for example by using a monthly figure where a daily figure was needed, or by forgetting a fixed charge that is added every month.

Worked example

On a flat tariff of 0.35 per unit, 300 units cost 105. On a tariff with a day price of 0.40 and a night price of 0.20, using 200 units by day and 100 by night costs 80 plus 20, or 100, slightly less for the same total use.

Common mistakes to avoid

People also go wrong with electricity tariffs by being too optimistic about their own behaviour. They assume they will save, drive, use or stream exactly the amount they planned, every month, without exception. Real life is messier, so build in a margin. Plan for the month when use is higher than usual, and check the result again after a month of real data. Another trap is rounding too early: keep two or three extra digits during the calculation and round only the final answer, because rounding at every step can drift the result by a surprising amount over a long period. Finally, do not trust a result you cannot explain back in one sentence. If you cannot say why the answer is what it is, check the inputs again.

  • Mixing up monthly and yearly figures in the same calculation.
  • Using a rate from another country or provider as if it were your own.
  • Forgetting a fixed charge, a fee, or a one-off cost that applies every period.
  • Rounding at every step instead of rounding only the final answer.
Tip: Check whether your tariff charges different prices at different times of day.

Frequently asked questions

How is my electricity bill calculated?

Your bill is usually the number of units (kilowatt-hours) you used, multiplied by the price per unit, plus a fixed charge. The bill calculator on this site follows that method, using the unit price you enter.

What is a kilowatt-hour?

A kilowatt-hour is the energy used by a 1,000-watt appliance running for one hour. A 100-watt bulb running for ten hours also uses one kilowatt-hour.

How can I lower my electricity bill?

Use less energy, especially from large appliances, and check your unit price and fixed charge. The energy-saving estimator shows what a change is worth in money.

Putting it into practice

Start today with your own numbers, not with a guess. Gather one bill, one meter reading or one recent statement, enter the figures carefully, and look at the range of answers rather than a single figure. If the answer is higher than you hoped, change one input at a time and see which change helps most. That is the whole method, and it works for savings, tax, fuel, electricity and data alike. A clear calculation, checked against real life, is the simplest way to make a confident decision.
